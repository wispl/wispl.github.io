## Introduction {#introduction}

As rockets fly higher and faster, the pressure in the atmosphere decreases. Recall that the pressure decreases with altitude. At a high enough altitude (say 10,000 feet), the pressure inside the rocket is greater than outside. The pressure inside is directed outwards, and looking a the nose cone, the pressure will be pushing it upwards at roughly around

\\[
 F = PA \quad\quad A = \pir^2
\\]

Usually, even before the 10,000 feet altitude, the **pressure separation** (might be called **drag separation**) will cause the nose cone to fly off. For rockets that don't fly super high or super fast, simply **drilling holes on the sides is enough**. The holes allow air to escape and balances the pressure.

Of course, for very fast rockets or ones which fly really high, the holes are not enough and so **shear pins** have to be used.

Shear Pins
: plastic or nylon rods or screws

These are used to hold the rocket together, but they generally weak so that the ejection charge will break them and allow the parachute to deploy. In other words, they are like a temporary binding for the rocket which gets discarded during ejection.


## Discussion {#discussion}

See [here](https://www.rocketryforum.com/threads/when-to-use-shear-pins.192427/) for discussions on when to use shear pins. One of the users. Notably **always use shear pins for rockets with separations**.
Interestingly enough, someone noted that shear pins are "rarely needed" for drag or pressure separation unless "there is a tremendous difference in mass/drag of the two sections"[^fn:1].

Besides guesswork, the two suggested approaches are using a [free body diagram](https://www.rocketryforum.com/threads/shear-pins-on-a-8-dia-bird.153740/post-2412413) or using OpenRocket to plot a deceleration post-burnout graph and then take the maximum Gs and multiply it with the forward section of the rocket. Use shear pins if friction is not strong enough&nbsp;[^fn:2]
