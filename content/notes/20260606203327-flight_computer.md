## Introduction {#introduction}

All a **flight computer** does is to open a parachute at the right time. Once the flight computer deems that it is time for deployment, it sends a signal to a device of your choosing (usually an igniter to ignite black powder) to deploy a parachute. The computer usually dinstinugishes between

1.  apogee event: highest point of rocket
2.  main event: at around 500-1,000 feet of altitude **during descent**

In some cases, like for a two-stage rocket, the flight computer should be able to determine between another two events: stage separation and second stage motor. Both are done via ignition. The user should be able to configure when each event occurs (like at what altitude main triggers).

There are some other interesting features

-   beeping and LED light flashes to signal how high the rocket went after landing
-   radio transmitter to broadcast altitude back to ground station
-   ability to download data from the flight, the data includes max speed, descent speed, and maybe battery level
-   beeps to tell if connections for drogue and main events are good (checks for continuity between igniters and altimeter) before launch
-   beeps loudly after launch to signal location

When a radio is included, a GPS usually is as well. This is useful for locating the rocket after a launch.


## Altimeters {#altimeters}

Flight computers generally include an altimeter. Historically accelerometers were used but they were much less accurate than barometers. And another historical fact was rockets tend to misfire because of incorrect reportings due to uneven pressure distributions caused by the sonic shockwave, but this is generally not an issue today. Currently, altimeters

-   uses a barometer to determine the altitude
-   might have use accelerometers to prevent the shockwave issue and also wind from tricking the barometer that the rocket has taken off (uneven pressure distribution)
-   might have an gyroscope


## Battery {#battery}

Generally a 9-volt or 12-volt alkaline battery is enough. The 12-volt one is generally for multistage rockets which requires a stronger igniter. Common igniters are **electric matchers (e-matches)** or the Quest Q2G2 igniters. Also if the flight computer supports it, usuing dual batteries is a good idea to increase volts and amps for delivering them to the igniters.

Apparently, Duracell batteries (for 9-volt batteries) are generally preferred because they have **soldered connections** for their interior six 1.5-volt batteries as opposed to **pressured connections**. The soldered connections are much more stable compared to the pressured one, and can retain the connection for when the rocket spins and tumbles rapidly.


## Redundancy {#redundancy}

Use two flight computers instead of one. In fact you generally want to have redundants of everything, so two computers, batteries, charges, and parachutes. Though in most cases, a single parachute is used (having a redundant for this is rather difficult). But it turns out that a lot of issues is due to the flight computer so having a redundant is already very good.

Some flight computers allow a small delay between apogee and apogee event. This essentially allows you to use a second computer as a backup in case the first one fails, for instance, you configure the delay on the second computer (around a second or less)

1.  first computer fires at apogee but fails
2.  the second computer fires at apogee after the delay

The second computer will have ensure the recovery is successful. And even if the first computer didn't fail, the second computer will just fire into the air (because of the delay) so no harm done. The delay is required since firing both charges at the same time might destabilize the rocket.

Similarly, for main event, you can just configure the second computer to trigger at an altitude lower than the actual main altitude (like 100 feet or more).


## Recommend Flight Computers {#recommend-flight-computers}

The RRC3 and RRC2+ are generally recommended and have much of the features discussed earlier. The RRC3 has an additional programmable event. These both supports altitudes up to about 40,000 feet.
